## 1. The process, start to finish

1. **Sourcing.** Find owners before they list: licensing lookups, local CPA society chapters, brokers who specialize in accounting practices, and referrals from bankers and attorneys. Your buy desk (PR #65) is where you log them.
2. **Initial screen.** Look for recurring revenue (monthly bookkeeping and annual returns), an owner close to retirement with no successor, no single client above about 10% of fees, and staff who will stay.
3. **NDA and CIM.** Sign the broker's NDA to get the CIM (the confidential information memo) and 3 years of tax returns and P&Ls. Off-market sellers usually have no CIM, so you build the picture from their returns and client list.
4. **Diligence.** The checklist below. In accounting, the core questions are whether the earnings are real and whether the clients will stay.
5. **LOI.** Non-binding: price, structure, seller transition period, exclusivity (often 60 to 90 days, estimate) and contingencies. Under SBA it must be a fixed price with no earnout.
6. **Financing.** SBA 7(a) through a Preferred Lender, plus a 10% injection, an independent valuation and 1.25x historical debt coverage. Details in section 5.
7. **Closing.** Asset purchase agreement, seller consulting agreement (up to 24 months under SOP 8.1), seller standby note, lender closing, and client and staff announcement letters.
8. **Integration.** Run the first tax season the seller's way, with the seller introducing you to the top clients. Move to the AI back office only after retention is clear.

### Diligence checklist

- [ ] **Quality of earnings:** rebuild SDE from tax returns and bank deposits; take out personal add-backs the seller cannot prove. *(Hire a QoE or CPA reviewer for anything near $500k, estimate.)*
- [ ] **Client concentration:** list the top 20 clients by fees; flag any above 10%. *(JofA notes very large clients may need longer retention periods.)*
- [ ] **Client retention history:** churn by year for 3 years, and how many clients are tied personally to the owner. *(The biggest risk in accounting deals.)*
- [ ] **Staff:** who does the work, their pay and tenure, and whether any key preparer holds a PTIN or CPA license. *(Retention bonuses are common, estimate.)*
- [ ] **Tax-season seasonality:** monthly revenue and cash for 24 months; plan for lean months from May to December. *(Affects working capital and DSCR timing.)*
- [ ] **Software stack:** QBO or Xero, tax software, client portal, document management; check license transferability and data export. *(This is where your AI edge starts.)*
- [ ] **Licensing and liability:** firm license status (TSBPA), any attest work, E&O insurance claims history, and IRS preparer compliance. *(See the Texas section below.)*
- [ ] **Data security:** written information security plan, as tax preparers are expected to keep one (IRS Pub 4557). *(Inherited data risk.)*

## Texas licensing: the rule that shapes everything

Verified: Texas Occupations Code 901.354 requires a majority of a CPA firm's financial interests and voting rights to belong to CPAs (Texas or other-state licensed). Non-CPA owners are allowed only as a minority. Each one must be actively involved in the firm, have no history of dishonest or felonious acts, and, as a Texas resident, pass a professional conduct exam and submit fingerprints (22 TAC 513.11). A Texas-resident CPA must be designated as responsible for the firm license.

Also verified: under 22 TAC 513.10(a), a licensed firm is required only for attest services (audits, reviews, some compilations) or for using titles like "CPA" or "CPA firm."

**What a non-CPA buyer can do**

- (+) Own 100% of a bookkeeping, payroll and tax-prep firm that does no attest work and does not call itself a CPA firm. Paid preparers each need an IRS PTIN.
- (+) Partner with a Texas CPA who holds the majority of a separate licensed firm for any attest work, while you own the non-attest operating company (estimate: this split is a common pattern; have a Texas attorney structure it).
- (+) Own a minority stake in a CPA firm if you meet the 513.11 conditions.

**What a non-CPA buyer cannot do**

- (-) Own a majority of a licensed CPA firm.
- (-) Keep "CPA" in the firm name after buying a non-CPA practice.
- (-) Perform or sell audits and reviews without a licensed firm.

The practical implication: Anchor's first target should be a non-attest bookkeeping and tax-prep shop, or a CPA practice where the attest work is small and can be referred out or kept by a CPA partner.

## 4. The AI edge, honestly

What Current reports (self-reported): its Tax AI pilot processed about 7,000 returns and cut preparation time by 31% on average. A 98% accuracy figure has also been reported for the same pilot. These are company claims, not independent audits.

| Workflow | What AI does | Human still |
|---|---|---|
| Client intake | Engagement letters, organizers and checklists generated from last year's return | Approves scope and pricing |
| Document collection | Portal requests and automated reminders by text and email for missing W-2s, 1099s and statements | Calls the slow 10% |
| Categorization | Suggests bank-feed categories and learns per client | Reviews exceptions |
| Reconciliation | Matches transactions and flags breaks monthly | Signs off on close |
| Tax prep data entry | Extracts source documents into tax software | Preparer reviews and signs |
| Client follow-up | Status updates, questions answered from the file, appointment scheduling | Handles advice and bad news |

**Risks and limits**

- (-) Savings at a small firm are an estimate. I found no independent source on what an AI back office saves a 3 to 10 person practice.
- (-) Errors carry liability. The signing preparer is responsible, so AI output needs review.
- (-) Client data is sensitive. Tax preparers are expected to keep a written security plan (IRS Pub 4557), and any AI vendor touching client data must fit it.
- (-) Older clients may resist a portal. Change too much in the first tax season and you lose the clients you paid for.
- (-) Being Gen Z helps with tooling, but owners and clients trust experience. Pair yourself with a credentialed preparer.

## 5. Deal numbers

| Item | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Small accounting practice price | About 1.0x annual revenue | accounting8 guide (advisor estimate) |
| Price vs. SDE | About 2.0x SDE | accounting8 guide (advisor estimate) |
| All small businesses under $1M (Q3 2026) | Median $250k price, 2.30x cash flow | BizBuySell Insight Report |
| SBA 7(a) maximum | $5M | SBA 7(a) page |
| Equity injection (initial acquisition) | 10% of total project cost | SOP 50 10 8 and 8.1 |
| Seller standby note | Counts as equity only if on full standby (no principal or interest for the loan term); with minority equity, capped at half the 10% | SOP 8.1 via Polsky, Palavir, Prencipe |
| Debt service coverage | 1.25x on historical earnings | SOP 8.1 via Polsky |
| Valuation | Independent valuation required; 7(a) loan capped at the valuation amount | SOP 50 10 8; Starfield & Smith |
| Seller after close | Consultant only, up to 24 months (was 12 under SOP 8) | SOP 8.1 App. 15 via Palavir |
| Earnouts | Prohibited on SBA deals; buyer rebates allowed | SOP 50 10 8 and 8.1 |
| Non-SBA practice sales: retention clawback | e.g. 1x revenue paid over 5 years, adjusted to clients retained after 2 years | Journal of Accountancy |
| Non-SBA: percentage of collections | Traditionally 20% down, then 20% of collections a year for 4 years | Michigan CPA society (MICPA) |
| UK practice sales | Often 50% at completion, 50% after 12 months subject to retention | ACCA; Draper Hinks |

The key conflict: retention clawbacks and percentage-of-collections deals are how accounting practices traditionally sell, but SBA forbids seller earnouts. On an SBA deal, use a fixed price, a seller note on standby and perhaps a buyer rebate tied to performance, which SBA allows. Confirm the rebate wording with the lender and an SBA attorney.
