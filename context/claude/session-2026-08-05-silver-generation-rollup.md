## The Jacobs fit, verified against the actual book (not assumed)

- **His explicit AI-industry filter (Ch. 2):** avoid industries AI hollows out
  (accounting, paralegal, insurance brokering, for-profit ed). Favor industries
  that still need a real human body in a real room: he names healthcare and
  homebuilding specifically. Eldercare passes this cleanly: the care itself is not
  automatable, but the admin around it (scheduling, billing, compliance, family
  comms) is exactly the AI wedge.
- **His fragmentation filter (Ch. 2-3):** mom-and-pop, low computerization, ripe for
  standardization: same shape as waste management pre-United Waste and equipment
  rental pre-United Rentals. Go where big incumbents overlook ("rural Mississippi,
  the Upper Peninsula of Michigan" in his words), that maps directly onto Guam and
  the outer islands versus mainland metro home-care markets already being rolled up
  by PE.
- **The mechanism (Ch. 3):** buy at a multiple below what you'll trade at, improve
  operations, repeat. Respect the seller (patient, pleasant, polite; never play hard
  to get; never renegotiate after a handshake). Standardize fast post-close: one
  platform, one KPI dashboard, one training curriculum.

## The honest correction: scale, not the thesis

Every deal in the book used public-company stock or a personal billion-dollar
credit line as acquisition currency (Hamilton Resources literally started with a $1B
line of credit). An SBA-financed deal is a single operator buying one cash-flowing
business: a search-fund-style first acquisition, not a rollup on day one. Jacobs
himself built up through smaller ventures first (Amerex, then Hamilton, then United
Waste) before the capital access existed for what people now think of as "the Brad
Jacobs playbook." The realistic sequence here is the same: one deal, proven
operationally, before a second.

## Financing reality, researched 2026-08-05 (not assumed)

- **SBA 7(a)** covers acquisitions up to ~$5M, requires a minimum 10% equity
  injection at change of ownership; a seller note can satisfy part of that only if
  structured to SBA spec from the start, not bolted on after.
- **Critical constraint for this specific thesis:** SBA does **not** finance
  Medicare-certified home health or hospice acquisitions, because the CHOW
  (change-of-ownership) timeline creates a sustained period of seller billing that
  breaks SBA's structure. SBA 7(a) works for **non-medical home care** up to ~$5M.
  This means the entity type matters enormously for financeability: a
  Medicare-certified SNF or home health agency is a very different (harder,
  non-SBA) deal than a non-medical companion-care/home-care business.
- **Guam-specific gap:** there is pending federal legislation (Rep. Moylan) to raise
  the SBA 7(a) guarantee to 90% specifically for territorial businesses, implying
  current lender participation in Guam is thinner than the mainland. The first real
  deal is likely more bankable sourced/closed in Hawaii (where the 10 named firms
  already are) even if the long-term footprint is Guam/Marianas.
- **The market's own multiple arbitrage, current:** sub-$2M EBITDA trades 3-5x,
  mid-market tuck-ins 5-7x, platform exits 8-12x. Buy at 4x, exit at 10x is the real
  version of Jacobs' "buy low multiple, sell high multiple" today.
- **Who's already rolling this up nationally:** Aveanna Healthcare, Compassus
  (TowerBrook/Ascension), Elara Caring (Bain/Kohlberg), Help at Home
  (Centerbridge/Vistria), all PE-backed platforms. This confirms the model works
  and confirms mainland metro markets are already taken. Guam/outer-island
  underservice is the actual opening, same as Jacobs' tertiary-market logic.

## The wealth-transfer context, researched 2026-08-05

Per McKinsey Institute for Economic Mobility (via Forbes, 2026): 6 million small and
midsize US businesses will change hands by 2035 as baby boomers retire; about 1
million will actually be sold, cumulatively worth $5 trillion. More than half of US
small-business owners are over 55 today (up 30% since the early 2000s); 72% of
boomer owners have no written succession plan. This is the demand side of the deal
flow this thesis depends on; it is real and it is not going away over the horizon
this plan needs.
